I often like to think about the history that's right under my nose; the kinds of stories that if
someone knew them, they'd be bursting to tell me about. It could be something like a fun bit of
trivia about how the street I'm on earned its name or how the actions of someone I never knew
about are central to why my life is the way that it is today. Here's one I found out recently: the
place I grew up in - [Hor al Anz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hor_Al_Anz#History) East - was
named after a sheikh's horse that died in that area and was buried in a sand dune. Weird, right?

While I was at Rice University, I spent some time in the short-lived Special Projects division of
the campus newspaper - The Rice Thresher. Our specialization was doing web-based storytelling of
the past and present of Rice. In the course of working there, I got to learn little vignettes
about the people who were here before me like the stories of [the women who lived through major
shifts in access to
abortion](https://projects.ricethresher.org/projects/abortion/a-history-of-abortion-told-through-rice-alumni),
the history of [gentrification in the surrounding
community](https://projects.ricethresher.org/projects/innovation/the-data-are-unclear-on-ions-gentrification),
and the fact that [in 2003 we won the National College Baseball
Championship](https://projects.ricethresher.org/projects/baseball).

It made me internalize the reality that there is a rich history to be understood about Rice,
though the four-year nature of undergraduate learning makes institutional memory hard to maintain.
I got into the habit then of occasionally wondering how students in the past reacted to major
historical events to get a sense of perspective about my place in history. I would often search
through an online archive of the Thresher, but I ran into an issue where the OCR model used on the
paper was fairly out of date, making it unlikely that many of my searches yielded accurate
results. It made that century-long archive of the past feel impenetrable.

Fast forward a few years and suddenly AI models in many domains have gotten really good. A few
months ago, I did some digging and found a state-of-the-art OCR model from Nvidia that was perfect
for extracting text from newspapers and similar documents. I also found a service that gave access
to GPUs in their downtime, so they cost 1/10th the price to rent. In a fit of inspiration, I
downloaded the full archive of the Thresher as well as the annual yearbook - the Campanile - and
transcribed them with this model for the full ~100 year history spanning 78,645 pages.

Once I had this much more searchable corpus of information, I started digging into it. I was able
to ask an agent like Codex or Claude Code to tell me about how students reacted to WWI and it
would respond by searching for keywords like "war", "soldier", "army", etc. in issues of the
Thresher and Campanile from the 1910s. It would identify the issues that were most relevant, read
them to understand the sentiment reflected in them, and then synthesize that information in a way
that I could explore further on my own. Here is an example of a question I posed and the final
response it synthesized:

Based on the responses it provided to my various questions, I probed the pages it identified as
containing relevant material to get a fuller picture of things and to ensure that an OCR mistake
wouldn't translate into a misunderstanding of the original text. I've got instructions for you to
be able to explore the archive on your own, but I can give you a sampling below of some of the
topics I was curious about and decided to explore.

When the COVID pandemic hit, it felt like the sort of thing that you would only have heard about
happening in the past. Of course, the Spanish flu had taken place about a century prior, and Rice
had been around during that time. Looking at the re-scanned archive, I found an echo of a feeling
many had during the more recent pandemic. I remember stifling every minor cough or sneeze for fear
of arousing suspicion that I was infected. Some developed hypochondriac tendencies that lasted
beyond COVID. I found this gem embedded in a 1919 issue of the Thresher that parodied a similar
paranoia from a different era:

In other searches, I learned that the namesake of the Heisman Trophy - John Heisman - was once a
coach at Rice. He had just come off a string of successful coaching gigs at other college football
programs, and he came to Rice with the intention of invigorating their football program. He wrote
several letters to the student body in which he laid out an affirmative vision for the changes he
wanted to bring to Rice's athletic program. In one of these letters, he effectively scolds the
Rice community for their lack of school spirit around athletics and their under-appreciation of
the student athletes. I don't think much has changed since 1924 in that regard.

He spent four years at Rice, only to end up with a record that he and others would regard as
unsuccessful. Heisman would not go on to coach another college football program.

Another thing that hasn't changed? Students complaining about campus food. An opinion piece
published in 1918 decried the "evils" present in the food system at Rice, which led to two "food
riots." Reading the piece makes it clear that they definitely had it worse back then. A 1922 piece
argued for reforms to combat inflexible meal times, and a 1968 piece complained that the food
quality had reached "new realms of inedibility." Students also seemingly have a rich tradition of
complaining about parking.

For all the protests Rice students have had against the quality of food and other features of the
campus, perhaps the most spectacular example is the Masterson crisis of 1969. When the students
and faculty learned that the Board of Trustees appointed a new president - William H. Masterson -
without their involvement, they protested so intensely that Masterson ended up resigning five days
after his appointment was announced. There's a great video by Melissa Kean that goes over what
happened:

The Thresher called it "one of the most significant episodes in the history of the University,"
which is probably true, and yet I think most students would be surprised to hear that such an
episode ever happened. They'd be more familiar with the Civil Rights Movement that had taken place
in parallel, a movement that made its most indelible mark on Rice in the form of the decision to
begin allowing Black students to attend in 1963/64.

Research from Rice's Task Force on Slavery, Segregation and Racial Injustice highlighted that the
push to desegregate was ["a decision spurred mainly by financial and aspirational motives rather
than a commitment to advancing civil
rights."](https://news.rice.edu/news/2020/legal-battle-over-desegregating-rice-gives-context-historic-decision)
According to a 1961 poll, the students and faculty were generally in favor of desegregation,
though a decent chunk of them were not, which is unsurprising considering the politics of the era.
A consequence of Rice breaking with the mandate of its charter to only admit white students was
that it would also no longer be tuition-free, which may have influenced how this vote went.

However, one thing that became clear as I continued exploring was that Rice has never been a
monolith. Some students at the time took offense to the display, and one in particular (the
then-president of ACCORD) wrote an opinion that dressed down the performance, albeit in language
that's now dated. Here we also see a recurrence of a pattern for college campuses that might be
familiar to us today: there was a backlash to the backlash in the form of an opinion from a
professor disagreeing with the show but arguing that their right to free speech should enable them
to perform it.

There's so much more to explore in this archive, and I've omitted many details for clarity and
brevity, but it's been a fascinating experience searching through it in natural language and
diving deep into topics to get a sense of what life was once like for a student like me before my
time.

If you do decide to dive into it like I did, it's important to keep in mind what we're looking at.
This archive is a time capsule, not a time machine: it's a record kept by people whose job was to
edit it in the process of preserving it. Occasionally you will see the explicit hand of the
editor, but even if it doesn't present itself that way, it's always there. It's also a record made
for the contemporary members of the Rice community, so the articles may gesture to an atmosphere
or context that the intended audience may be familiar with, but you will have to explore further
to understand that context.

I highly recommend checking out the collection of Thresher and Campanile editions, along with many
other records of history relating to Rice, over at the Woodson Research Center's Digital
Collections [official website](https://digitalcollections.rice.edu/). The archivists there have
done excellent work to compile these resources and publish them. They've also done the work to
showcase the kinds of stories of the Houston community that are too often untold, like in the case
of the reports from the [Task Force on Slavery, Segregation, and Racial
Injustice](https://digitalcollections.rice.edu/task-force-on-slavery-segregation-and-racial-injustice).

You can also read more stories collected by Rice's former Centennial Historian Melissa Kean at her
blog [Rice History Corner](https://ricehistorycorner.com/). I recommend her blogs about the time
that [A&M kidnapped Sammy the
Owl](https://ricehistorycorner.com/2014/04/24/the-kidnapping-of-sammy-1917/), or her writings
about the cast of characters who have been involved with Rice, which tell a deeper story about
their contributions to the university.

If you're more technically oriented, you can also explore the archive in the way that I initially
did from the OCR data I collected. I've published the portion of the archive that is in the public
domain here: [github.com/fadilf/rice-student-publications-ocr](https://github.com/fadilf/rice-student-publications-ocr).
You can either clone it from GitHub or paste the link into an online agent chat like Claude or
ChatGPT and ask it to explore the repository to answer your questions. You might be surprised by
what you discover. If you find anything interesting, please let me know!
